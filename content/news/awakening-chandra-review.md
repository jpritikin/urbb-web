When I first received a copy of *Religion Unburdened by Belief*, the title didn't make much sense to me. After all, people tend to treat belief as the core of religion, not an obstacle to it. But Pritikin's case is that the real work is returning to connection—with ourselves and with each other—and that this task calls not for dogma, but coherence in spiritual processes.

The word "connection" echoes what my own mentor has identified again and again as the drive underlying people's interest in psychedelics and spiritual practice in the first place. Pritikin points out that wanting connection isn't enough; you need a process for actually getting there. His book takes that seriously enough to identify a way to measure connection without self-report. It's one thing to sense something is true from years of client work, but it's quite another to see it argued with this much rigor. This is a rare book that reconciles the woo-woo with science in a way I find massively important to the collective conversation around the intersection of spirituality and human potential.

I also appreciated how the book handles a topic like spirit possession: setting aside the question of what's "objectively" happening to ask what actually helps. That's systems-thinking, and it reflects Pritikin's background working years as a software engineer before earning a PhD in quantitative psychology and then retraining in IFS. The systems-thinking shows up again in his analysis of how spiritual communities curdle into something exploitative. The book doesn't let a healthy wariness become an excuse to give up on community altogether, however, and he successfully addresses a multitude of red flags with standards that can easily be applied by any group of spiritual seekers.

I want to call out the brave section on Orgasmic Meditation, a partnered practice used as a route into altered states. Pritikin flagged it as optional, but I was glad it stayed in. A topic like sexuality that can barely be spoken about in the context of religion without devolving into moralizing and dogma tells you exactly how deep our content beliefs run, particularly around this subject; cutting it would have read as avoidance and instead it was addressed head-on.

I recommend this book wholeheartedly and especially to those called to spiritual leadership. It covers many sticking points that trip up people in that role, with more nuance and care than anything else I've read in this space. Given the scale of what we're up against with environmental degradation, political unrest, and nuclear threat, I believe that the highest-leverage change available to us is individuals finding more empathy and compassion for each other. Religion, done well, is one of the best places for that to happen—and especially if we're willing to think like Pritikin and courageously reimagine what that word actually means.
