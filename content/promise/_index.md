## Introduction

For decades now, there's been a persistent intuition running through psychedelic culture and research: that these substances could be a shortcut to a better world. The logic seemed simple enough. Give enough people a profound mystical experience, dissolve their ego for an afternoon, show them the interconnectedness of all things, and surely something downstream would shift. Less cruelty. More empathy. Better leaders, better parents, better therapists. The 60s counterculture believed this. Silicon Valley microdosers believe a quieter version of it. Even serious researchers at Johns Hopkins and NYU have leaned on language like "mystical-type experience" as if the experience itself were the mechanism of change.

Decades on, the evidence for that broad civilizational payoff isn't accumulating as fast as the early enthusiasm predicted. People have moving experiences on psychedelics, report profound insight, feel connected to everything, and then go back to work on Monday largely unchanged. Something about the "psychedelics as an awakening machine" theory was incomplete.

One possibility: observers were mistaking insight for the ability to act under pressure. So maybe the special opportunity these substances offer is a way to measure emotional regulation. It sounds like a small thing. How much damage, really, comes from people who got hijacked by a feeling and didn't notice until afterward?

## Problem Statement

Knowing what's right and doing it under duress are two different things. What do the following scenarios have in common?

- Therapists who use sessions to feed their own need to be needed.
- Teachers who mistake a frightened kid for a defiant one.
- Judges who sentence from irritation instead of cool judgment.
- Police who escalate because adrenaline is doing their thinking.
- Parents who pass their unprocessed trauma along to their children.

We have a word for what's missing. Several words, actually: empathy, compassion, emotional intelligence (EQ), emotional regulation, Self leadership. Different names, but roughly the same idea: noticing what you're feeling and choosing your response thoughtfully rather than being swept away by emotion.

## The Uncheatable Test

So how would you actually know if someone has EQ?

**Self-certification.** Someone tells you they're an empath, that they're "highly sensitive," that they've "done the work." Okay, but can you trust that evaluation? People most in need of the skill are often the ones most confident they already have it.

**Questionnaires.** The catch: a questionnaire measures vocabulary and stated attitudes, not behavior. Someone can score well while an angry part is still acting out underneath. There's no way for the instrument to tell the difference.

**A trained observer's judgment call.** Take therapists, since they're arguably the most important case: a skilled clinical supervisor, watching a therapist work, may notice what the therapist can't see in themselves. But this kind of evaluation is slow and expensive—someone with extensive training has to sit in the room, session after session. And it's still a judgment call. Share the same session with two different supervisors and you may get two different verdicts.

What these approaches share is that they're often evaluating someone in a calm state. A better test would reliably provoke emotion—their own material, arriving on its own terms—and watch what happens next.

Consider a classical psychedelic like N,N-DMT, psilocybin, or LSD. Among other effects, these substances make inner experience more vivid and enticing—part of why bad trips happen. Some of those trips go bad for mundane reasons: too high a dose, an unsafe setting, an inexperienced guide. Others go bad when fear, grandiosity, or an old wound unexpectedly takes over. Losing yourself to these feelings is almost irresistible.

Feeling that pull and not being swept away by it is the skill, one you can't just claim to have. Daily life provokes people too, but unpredictably: a crisis arrives on its own schedule, at whatever intensity it happens to bring. A psychedelic session administers a measured dose of provocation. Fail once and it might be something about the occasion. Fail consistently and it's you.
